Background and Beginnings

Background of the Revolt

The roots of the rebellion lay in deep-seated religious, economic, and political friction between the Jewish population of Judea and the Roman Empire. Decades after the destruction of the Second Temple, Roman rule under Emperor Hadrian grew increasingly oppressive. The local population suffered from heavy taxation and the confiscation of agricultural lands. Tensions reached a boiling point when Hadrian enacted strict decrees targeted directly at Jewish religious practices, most notably a sweeping ban on circumcision, which Jews viewed as a fundamental threat to their religious survival and identity.

How the Revolt Began

The immediate trigger for the uprising was Emperor Hadrian’s decision to rebuild Jerusalem as a pagan Roman colony named Aelia Capitolina, which included plans to build a temple dedicated to Jupiter on the site of the ruined Jewish Temple. In response, the Jewish population quietly organized a massive, unified underground resistance led by Simon bar Kokhba. Instead of facing the superior Roman army in open battle, the rebels launched a highly coordinated, surprise guerrilla war. They utilized a vast network of hidden subterranean complexes to ambush Roman garrisons, quickly driving the Roman forces out of the region and establishing a temporary independent Jewish state.

The Fighting and the Demise of the Revolt

The Brutal War of Attrition

At its peak, the rebellion achieved incredible success, completely liberating Judea and establishing an independent Jewish administration for over three years. Shocked by these defeats, Emperor Hadrian sent nearly a third of the entire Roman army to crush the uprising. The Roman forces engaged in a systematic and devastating war of attrition. They burned hundreds of villages, blockaded water sources, and methodically sealed off the underground hiding complexes, slowly wearing down the highly resilient rebel forces.

The Fall of Betar and Aftermath

The war reached its tragic conclusion in 135 CE with the brutal siege of Betar, the final mountain fortress and headquarters of the rebels. The stronghold eventually fell, resulting in the death of Simon bar Kokhba and the slaughter of thousands of his remaining followers. The Roman victory was absolute and catastrophic for Judea; hundreds of thousands of Jews perished, survivors were sold into slavery, and Emperor Hadrian officially erased the name of the province from Roman records, renaming it Syria Palaestina to sever the Jewish connection to the land.
